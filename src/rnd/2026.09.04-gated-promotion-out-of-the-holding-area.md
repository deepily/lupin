# Promotion out of the holding area is manager-only, and asks Rick from inside the method

**Date**: 2026-09-04
**Source**: Rick, by voice + two keypress rulings
**Build order**: **THIS BEFORE THE FLEET LIMITER** — his ruling, not mine
**Status**: approved to build; **not filed as a row** — see below
**Owner**: Pocholo · **Accountable**: María

> **Why a doc and not a task row.** The ticket ratio gate refused the filing — *14 created
> against 34 closed, opens below 0.40*. This is a P1 and the **P0 exemption was not used** to
> get around it; that shortcut has been declined four times tonight by three different people.
> Rick's instruction was *"let's keep moving"*, so the work starts from here and the row gets
> filed when the gate opens.

## His words

> *"In the spirit of creating structures that force us to follow policy — how do you feel about
> writing the method to promote something from the holding area into the live task list queue,
> that the caller's credentials are checked to make sure they're actually a manager? And if
> they are, the next thing that happens is that the method you call asks, on your behalf, me,
> if you can take a task out of the holding area and promote it into the queue. That would be
> a nice way to make it a structurally unavoidable blocker to workers — or managers — who just
> took it upon themselves to promote something without asking me."*

## What makes the design work — preserve this, it is the whole point

**The ask lives INSIDE the method.** Not *"the manager should ask Rick"* but *"the promotion
cannot happen without Rick being asked."* A worker is refused on credentials; a manager causes
Rick to be asked on their behalf. **There is no path that quietly skips him**, so the policy
stops depending on anyone remembering it.

Same move as the fleet limiter, one level up. Tonight is the argument for both: **six workers
ran against a verbal cap of four**, and three sessions read the same sentence three different
ways. Nobody was careless — the rule lived in people's heads.

## Behaviour

1. **Caller must be a manager.** A worker calling it is refused, and **the refusal names the
   cause** — a promote that fails without saying why sends someone hunting a bug that does not
   exist.
2. **If the caller is a manager, the method asks Rick.** Yes/no, **default YES**, per his
   standing rule that an absent Rick must not become a blocker. He keeps the veto when present
   and loses nothing when away.
3. **Record which way the answer came.** A keypress and a timed-out default must not look
   identical on the row — the ask surface already distinguishes them. Without this, nobody can
   later tell which promotions he actually blessed.

## ⚠️ The caveat Rick ordered documented — in his own words

> *"Just 'is a manager' is sufficient for right now. This is not like we're dealing with
> finances or editing genomes — we're simply promoting a task from one list to another. So
> document that just 'is a manager' is not quite foolproof. And then let's keep moving."*

**So the check is plain manager-only, and the documentation must say it is not foolproof, and
why:**

A credential check is only as strong as the identity underneath it. Tonight we proved a
process can **silently resolve as another seat** — a detached process launched from one
worker's seat came back as another persona's, with no error and no alert (row `54a43bcf`,
fixed at `13014bd1`). **A gate asking "are you a manager?" answers YES for a borrowed manager
identity.**

`13014bd1` already makes identity-bearing writes refuse a *guessed* identity, so the hardening
exists and is wiring rather than new work. **Rick has deliberately deferred it on a
proportionality judgement, not an oversight.**

⇒ Put the caveat **at the check itself**, in a docstring — not only in this doc — so nobody
later mistakes a deliberate deferral for something nobody thought of.

## Done means

- A worker calling promote is **refused**, with a reason naming the credential.
- A manager calling promote causes **Rick to be asked, default yes, from inside the method**.
- The row records whether approval was a **keypress or a default**.
- An arm goes **RED** if the credential check is removed, and another if the ask is bypassed.
- The not-foolproof caveat is documented at the check itself, citing the identity bleed.

⚠️ **An arm that never fails is not a guard.** Delete each control and watch the right arm go red.

## Two things the plan did not anticipate

**1. The dial.** The ask timeout was frozen at import as a constant. It is now read at call
time from config, matching the other approval keys, so an edit lands on the next promotion
rather than the next deploy. ⚠️ **The splainer entry says explicitly that the dial does not
decide WHETHER Rick is asked, only how long he has** — setting it to 1 makes him effectively
absent and every promotion self-approve, and it does *not* make the gate dark. The dial for
the gate itself is `task approval enforcement active`.

**2. The gate rides the SAME switch as the approver allowlist, deliberately.** With
holding-area enforcement off the room is not being policed at all, so asking Rick to bless a
promotion nobody is restricting is noise an operator cannot turn off from the one dial meant
to control this door. Two gates on one door with two switches is how a "disabled" feature
keeps interrupting somebody.

## ⚠️ The two checks agree by coincidence, not by construction

The approver allowlist above the gate is configuration — `mr radio, cheech, maria` today,
plus Rick unconditionally in code. **Nothing keeps it in step with manager-hood.** A NEW
manager absent from that list is refused by the allowlist and never reaches this gate at all.
Two predicates answering one question by different routes agree right up until their inputs
diverge; the caveat is recorded at the call site as well as here.

## What is NOT yet proven — read before reviewing

The 16 tests drive the **module**. Nothing yet drives the **assembled app**, so a revert of
the call site in `tasks.py` leaves every one of them green. That gap is named in this repo's
own doctrine (*implemented but not installed*) and closing it is the remaining work.
