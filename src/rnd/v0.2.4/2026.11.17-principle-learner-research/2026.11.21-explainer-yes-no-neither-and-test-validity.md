# Explainer: three answers plus a comment, and what that does to the accuracy tests behind the stop ruling

Cheech, 21 Nov 2026, about 22:15 Eastern. A preview for Rick; nothing here has been added to the negative-result paper (`src/rnd/v0.2.4/2026.11.17-principle-learner-research/2026.11.21-principle-learner-negative-result-paper.md`).
Sources: the two read-only reviews written tonight, `2026.11.21-addendum-yes-no-neither-review.md` and `2026.11.21-addendum-recommendation-directives.md`, both in this folder, and the paper's own tables in section 4.1. Counts are for the 1,926 cards of run 2 (769 yes-or-no, 1,157 multiple-choice) unless stated.

## The short version

- A yes-or-no card offers Rick three answers: yes, no and neither. Any of the three can carry a comment that qualifies it.
- The test gave every forecaster only two answers, yes or no, and scored Rick's answer with the comment stripped off.
- So the test measured something narrower than "can we forecast Rick's answer". It measured "can we forecast the yes-or-no part, on the cards where Rick picked yes or no".
- The stop ruling still holds for that narrower question. It was never tested on the wider one, which is the one the learner exists for.

## 1. What "without the neither cards, accuracy rises" meant

It was not a better analysis. It was the same analysis with a set of guaranteed misses taken out of the count.

| | Right | Cards counted | Accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| The fixed rules (always say yes), all yes-or-no cards | 621 | 769 | 80.8% |
| The fixed rules, the 108 neither cards removed | 621 | 661 | 93.9% |
| Plain Haiku, all yes-or-no cards | 542 | 769 | 70.5% |
| Plain Haiku, the 108 neither cards removed | 542 | 661 | 82.0% |

The number on top of the fraction does not move, because no forecaster got a single neither card right. Only the bottom moves. Rick's reading is correct: dropping the neither cards hides the problem; it does not solve it.

The honest way to include them is the opposite of dropping them. Let every forecaster answer yes, no or neither, and score all 769 cards. Nobody knows what that accuracy is yet, because it needs a re-run.

## 2. What the cards actually look like

Yes-or-no cards in the window, by Rick's answer:

| Rick's answer | Plain | With a comment | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yes | 504 | 117 | 621 |
| No | 11 | 25 | 36 |
| Neither | 2 | 106 | 108 |
| Free text, no stance | 4 | 0 | 4 |
| All | 521 | 248 | 769 |

Three things stand out.

1. **Neither is 14% of yes-or-no cards, and it nearly always comes with a reason** (106 of 108). It is not a shrug. It says "the question is framed wrong, here is why". That is a re-frame request, which is information.
2. **About a third of all yes-or-no answers are qualified** (248 of 769). A "yes, but do not touch the configuration" is a different answer from a plain yes. The test scored it as a plain yes, so a forecaster that said yes got full credit for an answer it only half predicted.
3. **"No" is rare** (36 cards). Most of Rick's pushback arrives as neither or as a qualified yes, not as a no. A two-answer test reads most of his pushback as either a miss or a yes.

Multiple-choice cards have the same shape in a different form. There is no comment box, so Rick uses the Other box for comments. Of the 528 multiple-choice cards where he did not pick the recommended option, 437 contain typed text. Those were scored as departures that no forecaster could ever match. One reviewer hand-read 40 of those typed answers:

| What the typed answer was | Of 40 | Share |
|---|---|---|
| The recommended option plus a comment | 10 | 25% |
| A modified recommended option | 1 | 3% |
| Another listed option plus a comment | 6 | 15% |
| A re-frame, a request for context, or "ask me later" (what neither means on a yes-or-no card) | 17 | 43% |
| A different answer altogether | 6 | 15% |

This is one unblinded reader's estimate, so read the shares as rough. But it means the "437 unreachable departures" are mostly not departures: about a quarter agree with the recommendation, and the largest group are neither answers in multiple-choice clothing.

## 3. What this does to the design of the tests

### 3.1 The answer space was too small

Every model forecaster (plain Haiku, the principles forecast, plain Opus, Opus with principles, Sonnet, Fable) went through one prompt and parser that accept only yes or no on a yes-or-no card. A neither reply would have been refused as unreadable. On multiple-choice cards they could only pick a listed option. So no forecaster could express a re-frame or a qualification, which together are a large share of what Rick actually says.

### 3.2 The scoring threw away the part of the answer that carries the most information

The scorer stripped comments. The qualification is where Rick's judgement lives. A plain yes tells you he agrees; a qualified yes tells you the condition. A test that cannot see the condition cannot reward a forecaster for predicting it.

### 3.3 The test was blind exactly where principles should help most

The principle writer was given Rick's comments when it wrote the principles. So any principle that captured "Rick re-frames this kind of question" or "Rick says yes but adds this condition" had nowhere to go. The forecaster reading it could only answer yes or no. The design let principles in on the evidence side and shut them out on the output side. That is a bias against the principles forecast, and its size is unmeasured.

### 3.4 The "ceiling" was partly built by the test

The paper says the room above the fixed rules is nearly empty. Part of that emptiness is the test's own construction. The 108 neither cards, the 248 qualified answers and the re-frames hidden in the 437 typed answers are exactly the room above "always say yes". The test could not see that room, so it reported it as empty.

### 3.5 The default is not the recommendation on yes-or-no cards

The yes-or-no tool's own default is "no", and an older rule asks for a safe default. So on cards that default to no and state a direction, the written recommendation is yes 210 times and no 11 times (a rough text match, spot-checked on four cards). The yes-or-no rows of the paper's section 7 treat the default as the recommendation, so they measure the safe-fallback convention, not the advice.

## 4. What this does to the validity of the earlier results

| Claim in the paper | Still valid? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The principles forecast lost to plain Haiku, 67 against 115 | **Yes, for the question as frozen** | Both were blind to neither and comments in the same way, so the paired count does not move. |
| The stop rule fired correctly | **Yes, as written** | The rule asked exactly that frozen question, and the answer stands. |
| Principles do not improve forecasts of Rick's answers | **Not shown** | The answers were reduced to yes or no, or to a listed option. The kinds of answer where learned principles could plausibly help were outside the test. |
| The fixed rules set a ceiling near 61.6% | **Overstated as a ceiling** | It is a ceiling for picking yes or the recommended option. A forecaster allowed to say neither or to predict a qualification is measured on a different, larger task. |
| The re-spin misapplication (two principles from one "no", retrieved on 120 of 127 cards) | **Yes** | It is a retrieval defect, independent of the answer space. |
| Timed-out defaults were not counted as Rick's answers | **Yes** | The 553 timeouts in the window have no answer time, and the loader keeps only answered cards. |
| The recommended option is chosen 89.2% of the time when Rick picks from the list | **Yes, but incomplete** | Many of his typed answers also agree with the recommendation, plus a comment. So agreement is higher than the paper says, and departures fewer. |
| The yes-or-no "follows the default" figures in section 7 | **Mislabelled** | The yes-or-no default is the safe fallback, usually no, not the recommendation. |

In one sentence: the stop ruling answered its question correctly, but its question was narrower than the learner's purpose, and the narrowing removed the very answers where principles were most likely to matter.

## 5. What a valid re-test would look like

1. **Three answers plus a qualifier flag on every yes-or-no forecast.** A forecaster returns yes, no or neither, and whether Rick will add a comment. Optionally it gives the gist of the comment.
2. **The same on multiple-choice cards.** A forecaster returns an option, "will re-frame", or "will add a comment".
3. **Re-label the stored typed answers once, before the run.** Sort each typed answer into option plus comment, re-frame or write-in. This could be done by a model judge, checked against a hand sample. Freeze the labels before any forecast is scored.
4. **Score in layers, with the layers frozen in advance:**
   - stance accuracy over three classes, the primary measure;
   - qualifier detection: did the forecaster predict that Rick would comment?
   - qualifier content, judged against the stored comment, as a secondary measure only, since it needs a judge.
5. **Two yardsticks, both frozen before the run.** Plain Haiku with the same three-answer prompt, and the best model-free rule. The rule should be extended to three answers, for example "yes, unless the card has a feature that historically draws neither"; fit that feature on cards before the window.
6. **Report the neither class on its own.** 108 cards is small. A forecaster that never says neither can still reach 86% on stance (661 of 769), so the neither cards need their own recall and precision, or the headline will hide them again.
7. **Cost.** Model calls only, on the stored archive: no new data, and no change to the cards Rick sees. It is about the size of run 2 (1,926 cards times the arms chosen), plus the one-time re-labelling pass.
