# Addendum: the directives that tell agents how to frame an ask, and what they can and cannot explain

Written 2026-11-21 by a read-only research pass. No tracked file was edited and no model or API call was made. Every count below was taken on 2026-11-21 from `io/phase4-archive-run-2/archive.sqlite`, opened read-only, over yes-or-no and multiple-choice cards created on or after 10 September 2026 (the paper's evaluation window).

## Verdict

Six directives in force for the whole window order the agent to mark one option recommended and put it first, and the cards obey: the single recommended option is first in 1,511 of 1,513 answered multiple-choice questions. That explains why nearly every card carries a recommendation and why the forecasters converge on it. It does not explain Rick's 50% whole-card follow rate, which is set by his typed answers. It also does not explain the yes-or-no "default", which the directives contradict each other on and which on most cards points the opposite way from the stated recommendation.

## Does the directive set explain the paper's numbers?

**Why a card carries a recommendation: yes, fully.** Rows 1, 4, 5 and 7 make a recommendation compulsory and forbid the neutral menu. Rows 2, 5, 9 and 11 put it first with "(Recommended)" in the label. The cards comply: one recommended option on 1,513 of 1,624 answered questions (93.2%; 87 had none and 24 had two or more), and that option is first in 1,511 of 1,513. So "the fixed rules", which pick the labelled option or the first one, are the same rule twice over.

**Why the forecasters agree with it about 97% of the time: mostly, but only through reading.** The directives put the word "(Recommended)" in the card text every forecaster sees, and they make the agent who wrote the card commit to its best guess. Either is enough to explain convergence. The directives cannot say which one the models act on, copying the label or reasoning to the same place. The paper's proposed no-owner test, re-running the plain forecasters with the label removed, is the one way to separate them.

**Why Rick follows it on only 50% of whole cards: no.** The directives govern what the agent writes and are silent on what Rick picks. The paper finds the gap is mostly his typing (437 of the 528 departed cards contain a typed answer). The directives do not cause or forbid that. Indirectly, the pros-and-cons rule may even invite it, because a card that names the trade-offs gives him the words to write a third way.

**The three stories in § 7.7 stay inseparable.** Under each story, the recommendation causes the choice, describes it, or shares a cause with it, and the directives produce the same card. They guarantee a recommendation is present and first. They say nothing about whether it moves him. Only the label-randomisation experiment in § 7.7 separates the stories.

**The yes-or-no "default" is not the recommendation, and the directives say two different things about it.** Row 3 says the default should match the recommendation. Rows 12 and 13 say a timeout should default to the safe "no", and the tool defaults to "no" when the agent passes nothing. On 2026-11-21 I scanned the 782 answered yes-or-no cards in the window that store a default, using a crude text match on "recommend… yes/no" in the abstract and message (spot-checked on four hits, all correct):

| Card default | Abstract recommends yes | Recommends no | Both | Mentions a recommendation, direction not parsed | No recommendation word |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| no (619 cards) | 210 | 11 | 4 | 218 | 176 |
| yes (163 cards) | 2 | 0 | 0 | 3 | 158 |

On the default-"no" cards where the direction could be read, the written recommendation was "yes" 210 times and "no" 11 times. So on yes-or-no cards the timeout default mostly records the safe choice the agent was told to fall back to, not its advice. That is why § 7.2 finds the "no" default followed only 5.5% of the time while "yes" is his answer 80.8% of the time. "Say yes" works because it is usually the written recommendation. The archive cannot tell whether a "no" default was passed on purpose or left to the tool's own "no", because both store the same value. That gap stays open.

## The timeout default

**Directives that tie the timeout default to the recommendation:** row 3 for yes-or-no ("The `default` parameter should match the recommendation") and rows 9 and 10 for the walkthrough's multiple-choice asks ("a `default` keyed by header = the recommended option's exact label (so a timed-out ask still lands on the recommendation …)"). No general directive requires a default on `ask_multiple_choice` outside the walkthrough. Measured: since 1 August 2026 (`fd11cd30b`) the server stores the default, yet only 211 of 1,180 answered multiple-choice cards in the window carry one. On those, the default equals the recommended option in 196 of 232 questions (84.5%).

**Could the archive count a timed-out default as Rick's answer? No, by the code and by the data.** `asks_from_session` in `src/cosa/research/preference_replay/extractor_replay.py` takes only notifications with `responded_at` set. A timeout writes `{"value": <default>, "source": "timeout_default"}` with state `expired` and leaves `responded_at` empty (`mark_expired` in `src/cosa/rest/db/repositories/notification_repository.py`). An offline default is stored as state `expired` with no answer (`_persist_response_required_sync` in `src/cosa/rest/routers/notifications.py`). A client-side multiple-choice timeout never writes to the database at all. In the window's archive:

| Type | State | `responded_at` set | Answer source | Cards |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| multiple choice | responded | yes | ui | 1,180 |
| yes/no | responded | yes | ui | 789 |
| yes/no | responded | yes | none stored | 1 |
| yes/no | expired | no | timeout_default | 526 |
| yes/no | expired | no | none stored | 151 |
| multiple choice | expired | no | none stored | 346 |
| multiple choice | expired | no | timeout_default | 27 |

So 553 timed-out defaults (526 yes-or-no, 27 multiple-choice) are out of the window, and every answered card carries source `ui`. The replay does not filter on `answer_source` itself. It is protected by the `responded_at` filter, not by a check on the source. `grep default_used` over `src/cosa/research/preference_replay/` and the paper returns nothing. The paper does not say how defaults were excluded, and it should.

A side effect worth stating: 1,050 of the window's 3,022 yes-or-no and multiple-choice cards (34.7%) expired without an answer from Rick: 553 with a stored default and 497 with none. All of them are excluded, and 1,970 (65.2%) were answered. The 377 yes-default yes-or-no cards that expired outnumber the 163 that were answered. These are mostly the re-spin cards that say "defaults to YES if away". So the evaluation sees the cards Rick chose to answer, which is a selection the paper does not name.

## What this would add to the paper (candidate addendum to § 7)

*§ 7.8 Why the recommendation is there.* The recommendation's near-universal presence is not a finding about the owner; it is mandated. Since May 2026 (before the window opened on 10 September) the agents writing these cards have been instructed in four places — the global session instructions, the voice workflow document, the voice server's connect-time instructions and the decision-walkthrough skill — to give pros and cons, name one recommended option, put it first and label it "(Recommended)", with a neutral menu explicitly prohibited; the cards comply (the recommended option is first in 1,511 of 1,513 answered questions). This accounts for part 1's "exists" and for the forecasters' convergence, since the label is in the text every forecaster reads, but not for the owner's 50% whole-card follow rate, which the directives do not touch and which is set mostly by his typed answers. Nor can it separate the three stories of § 7.7: each produces the same card. Two corrections follow. First, the yes-or-no "card default" in §§ 7.1–7.4 is not the agent's recommendation: one directive says the default should match the recommendation, but an older one and the tool itself default a timeout to the safe "no", and on the default-"no" cards whose abstract states a direction, the abstract recommends "yes" 210 times to 11. The yes-or-no rows of § 7 therefore measure the safe-fallback convention, not the recommendation, and "say yes" is the better proxy for the latter. Second, timed-out defaults never enter the evaluation (553 in the window, excluded because a timeout leaves `responded_at` empty), so no forecaster was credited for matching a default the owner did not choose; but the window is thereby the set of cards the owner chose to answer, roughly two-thirds of those asked.
