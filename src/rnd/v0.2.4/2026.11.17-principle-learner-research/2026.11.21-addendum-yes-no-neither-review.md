# Addendum review: did the negative-result paper read yes, no and neither the way Rick's cards work?

Reviewed 2026-11-21, read-only, against `src/rnd/v0.2.4/2026.11.17-principle-learner-research/2026.11.21-principle-learner-negative-result-paper.md` (sections 3, 4.1, 4.2, 7, 9), the run 2 archive `io/phase4-archive-run-2/` and the raw asks `io/write-ups/online-preference-learning/2026.11.18-notification-asks-raw.jsonl`. ## Verdict

The scorer reads all three values and strips comments correctly, but no forecaster in the run could say "neither". Its prompt and parser allow only yes or no, so the 108 "neither" cards (14.0% of the 769 yes-or-no cards) were charged as misses to every forecaster, and the paper never names them. Every paired count, the stop rule's 67 against 115 included, is unchanged, so the stop ruling stands. What moves is the accuracy levels and the paper's account of headroom. On multiple-choice cards, by contrast, the "typed" answers mix three kinds of answer: an option plus a comment typed into the Other box, a re-frame, and a real write-in. The paper treats all of them as one unreachable kind.

SQL (the window is the 769 yes-or-no ids from `records.jsonl`, loaded into a temporary table, with hyphens removed to match the stored ids):

```sql
-- create temp table w( id text primary key ); insert into w values( replace( '<uuid>', '-', '' ) ); ... x 769
with v as (
  select n.id, lower( trim( json_extract( n.response_value, '$.value' ) ) ) val, n.response_default dflt
  from notifications n join w on w.id = n.id
  where n.response_type = 'yes_no'
), c as (
  select id, dflt,
    case when val in ( 'yes', 'no', 'neither' ) then val
         when val like 'yes [comment:%'     or val like 'yes[comment:%'     then 'yes'
         when val like 'no [comment:%'      or val like 'no[comment:%'      then 'no'
         when val like 'neither [comment:%' or val like 'neither[comment:%' then 'neither'
         else 'unreadable' end stance,
    case when val like '%[comment:%' then 1 else 0 end has_comment
  from v
)
select stance, sum( has_comment = 0 ) bare, sum( has_comment = 1 ) with_comment, count(*) total,
       sum( dflt = 'yes' ) default_yes, sum( dflt = 'no' ) default_no
from c group by stance;
```

Two framings in the paper also change:

- **Yes-or-no headroom.** The fixed rules miss 148 yes-or-no cards: 108 "neither", 36 "no" and 4 free text. So 73% of the fixed rules' yes-or-no misses were cards no forecaster in the run could get right, because none was allowed to say "neither". The ceiling for any yes-or-no forecaster was 657 of 769 (85.4%), and the paper does not state it. Section 9's "plus yes-or-no departures" should say that only 36 of those cards are reachable for a forecaster that answers only yes or no.
- **Multiple-choice "typed" departures (the 437).** This is unmeasured at card level and estimated from the 40-field reading. About a quarter of the typed fields follow the recommendation in substance, and about two in five are re-frame requests. So the paper's "the 437 typed ... departures are unreachable for an option-picker by construction" holds under whole-card exact match. Its conclusion "it is the typing, more than the recommendation's quality, that caps multiple-choice accuracy" mixes three things. The per-field follow rate of 55.6% (825/1,483) would rise to roughly 64%, adding about a quarter of the 524 typed fields. The whole-card follow rate of 50.1% would also rise, by an amount I could not measure, because a card counts as followed only when every typed field on it endorses the recommendation. These are estimates, not re-derivations.

## Does the stop ruling's basis hold?

**Yes.**

- **"Neither" cards.** No forecaster was right on any of the 108, so none of them is a card where the two forecasts disagree. The stop rule's 182 disagreeing cards (67 against 115) are identical under either reading. The same holds for the comparisons with the fixed rules (20 against 182) and with plain Opus.
- **Multiple-choice write-ins.** Suppose the write-ins that endorse the recommended option were re-scored as following it. Among the 437 typed cards, the principles forecast picked the recommended option everywhere while plain Haiku did not on 15 cards, and the reverse happened on 34. In the worst case for the stop rule, all 15 become principles-only wins and none of the 34 counts, giving 82 against 115 (p = 0.022). The rule still fires, at ten or more disagreeing cards.
- **The fixed rules.** Against the fixed rules, the principles forecast picked the recommendation on none of these cards where the rules did not, and the fixed rules did so on 56 where the principles forecast did not. So any re-scoring of that kind widens the fixed rules' lead.

One basis does change. The paper's claim that the room above the fixed rules is "nearly empty" was measured with forecasters that could not output "neither". It is true for a yes-or-no forecaster. For a forecaster allowed to say "neither", it is unmeasured: up to 108 yes-or-no cards, and an unknown share of the multiple-choice write-ins that ask for re-framing, were never attempted.

## Candidate addendum paragraph

> **Addendum (21 November): the third answer.** The owner's yes-or-no cards take three answers, yes, no or neither, and any of them may carry a comment. "Neither" means the question needs re-framing. The scorer reads all three and scores a comment-bearing answer on its stance alone, so a "yes [comment: ...]" counts as yes. But no forecaster in this run could answer "neither": the model prompt allows only yes or no, the fixed rules always say yes, and the lookup engine abstains on a "neither" majority. In the evaluation window 108 of the 769 yes-or-no cards (14.0%; 106 of them with a comment) were answered "neither", and all 108 are misses for every forecaster. Dropping them leaves every paired count unchanged, including the stop rule's 67 against 115, and raises the yes-or-no accuracies: the fixed rules go from 80.8% to 93.9%, plain Haiku from 70.5% to 82.0% and the principles forecast from 67.4% to 78.4%. They also account for 108 of the fixed rules' 148 yes-or-no misses. Multiple-choice cards have no comment box, so a qualified pick reaches the archive as a write-in. In a hand reading of 40 write-ins, about a quarter were the recommended option plus a comment and about two in five asked for re-framing. The "437 typed" departures therefore mix agreement, re-framing and true write-ins. The stop ruling is unaffected. Whether a forecaster that may predict "neither" or a re-frame has room above the fixed rules was not tested.
