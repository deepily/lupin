# Preference learning algorithms for human-in-the-loop decision routing

**Three algorithms stand out for learning approve/reject preferences in low-data, online settings without GPU: Bayesian online logistic regression with Thompson sampling, Gaussian Process preference learning with BALD-based active querying, and case-based reasoning with Bayesian confidence overlays.** Each is grounded in published research, handles uncertainty natively, supports incremental learning from binary feedback, and runs comfortably on CPU in a Python/FastAPI stack. The choice between them depends on whether you prioritize implementation simplicity (Bayesian LR), data efficiency and uncertainty quality (GP), or cold-start interpretability (CBR). A hybrid architecture combining elements of all three provides the strongest practical system.

---

## 1. Bayesian online logistic regression with Thompson sampling

This is the strongest starting point: a Bayesian logistic regression (BLR) model provides the preference backbone, while Thompson sampling (TS) provides the exploration-exploitation strategy that governs when to act autonomously versus defer.

**Core mechanism.** BLR places a Gaussian prior over weight vector θ and models approval probability as P(approve | x) = σ(θᵀx), where x is a feature vector for the decision context and σ is the logistic function. After each approve/reject observation, the posterior p(θ | D) is updated via Bayes' rule. Since the logistic likelihood isn't conjugate to a Gaussian, the posterior is approximated using **Laplace approximation** — fitting a Gaussian at the posterior mode with covariance from the inverse Hessian. McCormick et al. (2012) developed a fully online variant using Kalman-filter-style recursive updates with **O(d²) per-step cost** via the Sherman-Woodbury-Morrison formula.

**Thompson sampling wraps this model to handle deferral.** At each decision, sample θ̃ from the current posterior, compute p̃ = σ(θ̃ᵀx*). If p̃ exceeds a threshold, act autonomously; otherwise defer. The stochasticity of posterior sampling automatically balances exploration (trying uncertain actions to gather feedback) with exploitation (acting on confident predictions). When uncertainty is high, samples spread widely, triggering frequent deferral and rapid learning. As the posterior concentrates, deferral frequency drops naturally — **precisely the progressive autonomy schedule the proxy system needs**.

**Data requirements are modest: 10–30 labeled examples** produce a usable posterior, with the prior preventing degenerate predictions even earlier. For a feature space of dimension d, roughly O(d) to O(10d) samples are needed for the posterior to concentrate meaningfully. Dwaracherla et al. (2024, ICML) demonstrated that Double Thompson Sampling requires **nearly an order of magnitude fewer queries** than passive exploration for equivalent performance.

**Uncertainty and deferral** are handled through two complementary signals: (1) the posterior predictive probability P(approve | x*) being close to 0.5 indicates the model is uncertain about this specific decision, and (2) the posterior variance x*ᵀΣx* being large indicates the model hasn't seen enough similar decisions. Either triggers deferral.

**Implementation complexity is low** — roughly 100–200 lines of NumPy/SciPy for the Laplace BLR, plus 20 lines for Thompson sampling on top. Update and prediction both run in **sub-millisecond time** for typical feature dimensions (d < 500). No external ML library is strictly required, though PyMC or NumPyro can provide a full Bayesian treatment if desired.

**Key papers:**
- McCormick, Raftery, Madigan & Burd (2012), "Dynamic Logistic Regression and Dynamic Model Averaging," *Biometrics*
- Russo, Van Roy, Kazerouni, Osband & Wen (2018), "A Tutorial on Thompson Sampling," *Foundations and Trends in ML*
- Dwaracherla, Asghari, Hao & Van Roy (2024), "Efficient Exploration for LLMs," *ICML 2024*
- Wu & Liu (2016), "Double Thompson Sampling for Dueling Bandits," *NeurIPS 2016*

**Strengths:** Simplest principled implementation. Fastest inference. Interpretable weights reveal which features drive preferences. Theoretical regret bounds from the TS literature guarantee convergence. **Weakness:** Assumes a linear decision boundary in feature space, so it may miss nonlinear preference patterns unless features are carefully engineered.

## 2. Gaussian Process preference learning with active querying

GP preference learning is the most **data-efficient** approach and produces the **best-calibrated uncertainty estimates**, at the cost of somewhat higher implementation complexity.

**Core mechanism.** A Gaussian process prior is placed over a latent utility function f: f ~ GP(0, k(x, x')), where k is a kernel (typically RBF or Matérn). The probability of approval is modeled via a probit link: P(approve | x) = Φ(f(x)). For pairwise preferences, Chu & Ghahramani (2005) showed that P(xᵢ ≻ xⱼ) = Φ((f(xᵢ) - f(xⱼ))/√2), with a preference kernel k_pref that enforces the antisymmetric structure of preference relations. Posterior inference uses Laplace approximation or Expectation Propagation. Benavoli et al. (2021) showed the exact posterior is a Skew-GP, enabling rejection-free sampling.

**The BALD criterion makes this approach actively data-efficient.** Houlsby et al. (2011) introduced Bayesian Active Learning by Disagreement: BALD(x) = H[p(y|x, D)] − E_{p(θ|D)}[H[p(y|x, θ)]]. This selects queries where posterior samples maximally disagree — precisely the points where human feedback is most informative. For GPs, BALD has a closed-form approximation using the posterior mean and variance. **This criterion doubles as a deferral mechanism**: decisions with high BALD scores are ones the model should ask a human about rather than handle autonomously.

Biyik, Sadigh and collaborators at Stanford built an entire active preference learning framework on this foundation. Their APReL library (2022) implements mutual-information-based active querying with batch selection via Determinantal Point Processes. Their experiments show convergence in **10–30 actively selected queries** — 2–5x fewer than random querying. The GP variant (Biyik et al., 2020, RSS) extends to nonlinear reward functions, with the GP posterior providing automatic uncertainty quantification.

**Data requirements are the lowest of any approach: 2–5 preference observations** produce non-trivial predictions thanks to the kernel's inductive bias. The kernel encodes that similar decision contexts should have similar approval probabilities, enabling structured generalization from minimal data. For moderate-dimensional problems (d ≤ 20), **20–50 observations** typically yield a well-calibrated model.

**Uncertainty is the GP's native language.** The posterior variance σ²(x*) directly quantifies epistemic uncertainty at any point. The system defers when σ²(x*) exceeds a threshold, or when the predictive P(approve | x*) falls within an uncertainty band around 0.5. Unlike BLR, GPs provide uncertainty that is naturally **input-dependent** — the model knows it's uncertain in regions of feature space it hasn't observed, even if it's confident elsewhere.

**Implementation uses existing libraries.** The prefGP library (Benavoli & Azzimonti, 2024) implements 9 GP preference models in JAX/PyTorch. GPro provides probit preference GPs with Bayesian optimization. BoTorch from Meta offers a production-grade PairwiseGP. Simpson & Gurevych's GPPL (2020) scales to thousands of items via stochastic variational inference. Standard GP computation is **O(N³) for training** where N is the number of observations — completely tractable for the few-hundred-observation regime this system operates in. Prediction is O(N²) but takes milliseconds for small N. All run on CPU.

**Key papers:**
- Chu & Ghahramani (2005), "Preference Learning with Gaussian Processes," *ICML*
- Houlsby, Huszár, Ghahramani & Lengyel (2011), "Bayesian Active Learning for Classification and Preference Learning"
- Biyik & Sadigh (2018), "Batch Active Preference-Based Learning of Reward Functions," *CoRL*
- Biyik, Huynh, Kochenderfer & Sadigh (2020), "Active Preference-Based Gaussian Process Regression for Reward Learning," *RSS*
- Biyik, Talati & Sadigh (2022), "APReL: A Library for Active Preference-based Reward Learning," *HRI*
- Simpson & Gurevych (2020), "Scalable Bayesian Preference Learning," *Machine Learning*

**Strengths:** Most data-efficient. Best uncertainty calibration. Nonparametric flexibility captures complex preferences without feature engineering. BALD provides near-optimal active query selection. **Weakness:** O(N³) scaling limits the case base to ~1,000–5,000 without sparse approximations. Kernel selection matters. Higher implementation complexity than BLR (~300–500 lines, or use a library).

## 3. Case-based reasoning with Bayesian confidence scoring

CBR is the most pragmatic choice for a system that must work from day one, provide human-readable explanations, and grow incrementally. It is also the closest conceptual match to the existing keyword-matching implementation, offering a principled upgrade path.

**Core mechanism.** CBR stores every past decision as a case: (context features, proposed action, human verdict). When a new decision arrives, it retrieves the k most similar past cases using a weighted similarity metric, then predicts the likely verdict based on retrieved case outcomes. The 4R cycle (Aamodt & Plaza, 1994) — Retrieve, Reuse, Revise, Retain — maps directly onto the proxy workflow: retrieve similar past decisions, reuse their outcomes as a prediction, revise if the human corrects, retain the new case for future reference.

**Deferral emerges naturally from retrieval quality.** CBR provides a built-in, intuitive confidence metric: confidence = max_similarity × verdict_consistency, where verdict_consistency measures agreement among retrieved cases. Three conditions trigger deferral: (1) no cases exceed a similarity threshold (novel territory), (2) retrieved cases have mixed approve/reject verdicts (ambiguous territory), or (3) the case base is too small for the decision type. This mirrors how a human proxy would reason: "I haven't seen anything like this before" or "past similar cases went both ways."

**No minimum data is required.** The system starts by deferring 100% of decisions and builds its case base organically from each human interaction. With **5–10 cases**, it can make tentative predictions on highly similar decisions. By **20–50 cases**, it has reasonable coverage of common decision types. Each new case immediately improves the system — there is no batch retraining step.

**The Bayesian overlay adds principled uncertainty.** Rather than relying solely on nearest-neighbor voting, a lightweight Bayesian logistic regression can be trained on the features of retrieved cases, weighted by similarity. This combines CBR's structured retrieval with BLR's smooth probability estimates and calibrated uncertainty. The posterior variance from this local regression indicates whether to trust the CBR prediction or defer.

**Interpretability is unmatched.** Every autonomous decision comes with a natural explanation: "I approved this because the 3 most similar past decisions (similarity: 0.94, 0.89, 0.85) were all approved by you." This is critical for building user trust in a proxy system and for debugging when the system makes errors.

**Implementation is straightforward.** CBRkit (Lenz, Malburg & Bergmann, 2024, ICCBR) provides a modern Python CBR toolkit with custom similarity measures, JSON/CSV loaders, and evaluation metrics — `pip install cbrkit`. Alternatively, a from-scratch implementation is ~150–200 lines: store cases in a list, compute weighted cosine/Euclidean similarity at query time, vote among top-k. Retrieval cost is O(n×d) for n cases and d features, easily handled by approximate nearest-neighbor libraries (FAISS, Annoy) as the case base grows.

**Key papers:**
- Aamodt & Plaza (1994), "Case-based reasoning: Foundational issues, methodological variations, and system approaches," *AI Communications*
- Smyth (2007), "Case-Based Recommendation," in *The Adaptive Web*, Springer
- Lenz, Malburg & Bergmann (2024), "CBRkit: An Intuitive Case-Based Reasoning Toolkit for Python," *ICCBR 2024*
- Burke (2000), "A Case-Based Reasoning Approach to Collaborative Filtering," *EWCBR*

**Strengths:** Works from first interaction. Maximally interpretable. Zero training latency. Natural cold-start handling. Simplest upgrade path from current keyword-matching system. **Weakness:** Feature engineering is critical — similarity metric quality determines everything. No gradient-based optimization of similarity weights (though CBRkit supports learned measures). Retrieval scales linearly without indexing. Less theoretically elegant than Bayesian approaches.

## Other principled approaches worth considering

Several additional methods merit attention as complements or alternatives:

**Contextual Thompson Sampling (bandits framing)** treats the proxy system as a contextual bandit where arms = {act autonomously, defer to human} and binary feedback is the reward. MABWiser (from Fidelity) provides a production-grade Python library with LinTS, LinUCB, and Beta-Bernoulli TS. Gao et al. (2021, IJCAI) formalized human-AI routing under bandit feedback, showing that joint optimization of the routing policy outperforms two-stage approaches. This is the simplest formulation but loses the structured preference model.

**Conformal prediction as a deferral wrapper** provides distribution-free guarantees regardless of the underlying model. Wrapping any of the above classifiers with conformal prediction produces prediction sets — {approve}, {reject}, or {approve, reject} — with finite-sample coverage guarantees. When the prediction set contains both classes, the system defers. MAPIE (`pip install mapie`) makes this a ~50-line addition. The catch: it requires **50–100+ calibration examples** for tight prediction sets, so it's a Phase 2 addition rather than a cold-start solution.

**Bayesian Bradley-Terry models** are the theoretical foundation of RLHF reward modeling. Caron & Doucet (2012, JCGS) developed efficient Gibbs samplers for Bayesian BT that work with small samples, providing posterior uncertainty over rankings. For the binary approve/reject case, BT reduces to Bayesian logistic regression, making this approach equivalent to Contender #1 when there's a single underlying preference dimension.

**Learning-to-defer frameworks** from Mozannar & Sontag (2020, ICML) provide consistent surrogate losses for jointly learning a classifier and a deferral policy. Their approach adds a (K+1)-th "defer" class to the output, trained with a loss proven to converge to the Bayes-optimal deferral policy. This is theoretically clean but requires labeled examples of expert decisions — a constraint that may not hold in the proxy system's early phase.

## How these three contenders map to a phased deployment

The three algorithms naturally compose into a progressive autonomy architecture:

**Phase 0 (0 observations): Pure deferral.** The system routes all decisions to the human, building its case base. CBR is the only component that can contribute — by storing cases for later retrieval.

**Phase 1 (1–30 observations): CBR-driven with active learning.** CBR begins making tentative predictions on highly similar cases. GP preference learning, initialized with a broad kernel, uses the BALD criterion to identify which decisions are most informative to ask the human about. The system defers on ~80–90% of decisions but asks strategically.

**Phase 2 (30–100 observations): Bayesian LR + Thompson sampling.** With enough data for a meaningful posterior, BLR provides smooth probability estimates. Thompson sampling governs the transition from deferral to autonomous action. CBR provides interpretable explanations for each decision. The system handles ~40–60% of decisions autonomously.

**Phase 3 (100+ observations): Full autonomy with conformal guarantees.** Conformal prediction wraps the BLR model, providing statistical guarantees on prediction quality. The system handles ~80–95% of routine decisions, deferring only on genuinely novel or ambiguous cases. GP-based active learning identifies remaining areas of uncertainty.

All three components run on CPU, require no GPU inference, and integrate cleanly into a Python/FastAPI backend. The total implementation — CBR case store, online Bayesian LR with Laplace approximation, Thompson sampling decision policy, and conformal prediction wrapper — is achievable in roughly **500–1,000 lines of Python** on top of libraries (CBRkit, SciPy, MAPIE), with sub-millisecond decision latency.

---

## Conclusion

The research literature offers a rich set of principled alternatives to ad-hoc heuristics for learning human preferences online. **Bayesian online logistic regression + Thompson sampling** provides the best simplicity-to-rigor ratio and should be the backbone of any implementation. **Gaussian Process preference learning with BALD** delivers maximum data efficiency for the critical early phase when every human interaction must count. **Case-based reasoning** provides the interpretability and cold-start robustness that a trust-sensitive proxy system demands. The three are not competitors — they are complementary layers of a system that starts cautious, learns fast, and earns autonomy through principled uncertainty management. The key architectural insight is that deferral is not a failure mode to minimize but an **information-gathering mechanism** that Thompson sampling and BALD can optimize to accelerate the path to trustworthy autonomy.
