## Quarter 1 — Accessibility and affordance

### The motivation

I can type. Slowly, and it hurts — ten to twenty words a minute on a good day. That's why my
shell is a thicket of aliases and single-key shortcuts I fire with my right hand: I can touch
the keyboard, I just can't touch it much, or quickly.

That distinction is the whole design brief. Typing isn't impossible for me — it's
**expensive**, and I pay for it in pain. So the question that started everything: can someone
like me drive an agent factory, a harness, and a platform as a **first-class user** — not as a
special case, not through an accessibility bolt-on, but as the person the system was designed
for?

Answering that honestly forces you to rebuild the whole interaction surface, because voice
is not a skin over a text UI. It is asynchronous, it is slow, and it has no scrollback.

### What that required

**1. A voice-in / voice-out layer.**
Microphone → ASR → intent routing → agent execution → TTS → dual-channel WebSocket → browser.
Voice runs end to end, including into and out of developer tooling.

**2. An API for staying in touch across long-running processes.**
Voice out alone is not enough. Agents run for minutes or hours; the user walks away. So the
notification layer is not a speaker — it's a messaging API: agents announce, the user is
reachable from across the room or from a phone, and nothing is lost when nobody was
listening.

**3. Dynamically rendered output — progressive disclosure.**
Not everything should be spoken. The rendering model gives the listener a choice of depth:

| Layer | What it carries |
|---|---|
| Spoken (TTS) | the headline — by default, roughly the first quarter of the content **\*** |
| Notification history | the remaining bubbles, readable at leisure |
| Abstract card | rich detail: tables, numbers, structure — never spoken |
| Document link | full drill-down into the underlying document |

The user chooses how far down to go. The agent doesn't decide for them by dumping
everything into the audio channel.

**\* That quarter is a runtime setting, not a fixed cut.** Turn it up and the whole
notification is spoken; leave it down and you get the headline with the rest available on
playback whenever you want it. The depth is the listener's dial, not the author's decision —
which is the same principle as the layers themselves, applied one level down.

> **Worth saying out loud, briefly — the architecture generalizes past me.**
> Every layer above is already text with a spoken rendering and an adjustable depth. Turn that
> dial to maximum and hold it there, and what you have is a screen reader. **A full-screen
> reader for vision-impaired users is a few tweaks from what already runs** — not a rewrite,
> because the hard part (decoupling content from how much of it gets spoken) is the thing that
> was built first.
>
> I built this for a motor disability. The same surface serves a sensory one, and that isn't
> luck — it's what you get when you treat voice as an architecture instead of a feature. Say
> it as an aside, not a promise: it is not built, and I'd rather claim the mechanism than the
> product.

**4. A generic interrogation and rendering framework.**
Agents need to ask, not just tell. The notification API carries a question surface that
covers the real shapes a question takes:

- yes / no (with a third "neither — re-frame the question" answer)
- open-ended
- multiple choice, exclusive
- multiple choice, inclusive
- batched open-ended questions on a single screen

**5. Context-carrying decisions.**
The hard part isn't the widget — it's that the user is almost never thinking about the thing
being asked. If I'm working on task A and an agent asks about task B, a bare menu is
useless. So every decision ships with **pros and cons per option and an explicit
recommendation**, recommended option first. That's what makes a cold task-switch cheap
enough to answer from across the room.

### Demo candidates

- Drive a real task by voice, from across the room, screen-free
- A long-running job that reaches out mid-flight and gets answered by voice
- A context-carrying multiple-choice decision — show the pros/cons/recommendation card

### 🎙️ *"What happens when ASR mishears?"*

> **Answer**: *"An optimized Whisper Large is incredibly good, so it's rare. When it does
> mishear me, Claude usually reads straight through it. And when it can't, it just sends me
> a notification asking what I meant — the LLM's version of 'Huh?'"*

**Three layers, and none of them is an error handler:**

| Layer | What catches the miss |
|---|---|
| **1. Transcription** | Optimized Whisper Large — good enough that misses are rare, not routine |
| **2. Comprehension** | The consumer is a language model, so it reads intent through a garbled word the way a person does |
| **3. Clarification** | It asks. Same notification surface as every other question — *"the LLM's version of 'Huh?'"* |

**The insight worth stating out loud, because it's the reason any of this works:**

> *A command parser needs the exact words. A language model needs the intent. Once the thing
> on the other end of the microphone can ask "did you mean X?", transcription stops needing
> to be perfect — it only needs to be close.*

That is why voice-first became viable now and not five years ago, and it's a better answer
than any accuracy statistic. **Every prior generation of voice UI failed at exactly this
seam**: mishear the word, miss the command, and the user repeats themselves louder. Nothing
in the stack could recover, because nothing in the stack understood.

**Note that layer 3 is not a special path.** The clarification uses the same question surface
described above — yes/no, open-ended, multiple choice, and the *"neither — re-frame the
question"* third answer. There is no separate ASR-error subsystem, and that's the point:
**a mishearing is just another thing an agent doesn't know yet, and agents already had a way
to ask.**

**Delivery**: keep *"the LLM's version of 'Huh?'"* verbatim. It gets the laugh and it
teaches the mechanism in five words — the room instantly knows what the interaction feels
like, which no description of a confidence threshold would achieve.

#### The unexpected side benefit — say this one, it's the part people feel

> *"I don't have to talk like an idiot anymore. No more Sirification. No more Alexafication.
> I stopped flattening my English so the machine could keep up."*

Everyone in that room has done it: the clipped, over-enunciated, subject-verb-object voice
you adopt for a smart speaker, stripped of clauses and contractions and anything resembling
how you actually talk. **It's a small humiliation performed dozens of times a day, and
nobody names it.** Name it.

**Why it belongs here and not in a features list:** it's the same architectural point as
the "Huh?" mechanism, arriving through the front door instead of the back. Old voice UIs
made *you* do the error correction — pre-emptively, by speaking a degraded dialect they
could parse. **The accommodation ran the wrong direction.** Good transcription plus a model
that understands intent means the system meets you at your own register: long sentences,
asides, self-corrections mid-thought, the way you'd talk to a colleague.

**And the accessibility tie-back, which is why this is more than a nice-to-have:**

> *A system that only understands you when you simplify yourself is not accessible. It just
> relocated the work.*

For a user who can't fall back to a keyboard, "speak in the machine's dialect" isn't a minor
annoyance — it's the accommodation being demanded of the person again, one more time, in one
more place. **The whole argument of this quarter is that the machine should do the adapting.
This is the most personal example of it, so it lands hardest.**

## Quarter 2 — Planning is prompting, and when human-in-the-loop becomes too much

### The motivation

Every agentic platform preaches human-in-the-loop. Run it for a year at real volume and you
discover the other failure mode: **sometimes human-in-the-loop is too much.** The human
becomes the bottleneck, rubber-stamps to keep moving, and their judgment gets spent on
trivia instead of on the two decisions that actually mattered.

### The answer: cascaded review

A multi-persona review pipeline that wraps the plan-review workflow. Its stated design goal,
verbatim from the workflow document:

> "Save the user's attention as a scarce resource by spending compute instead."

It does two things at once:

1. **Raises plan quality** — several independent reviewers, each with a distinct rubric
2. **Takes me out of the loop** — the manager filters findings; only genuine forks escalate

### The shape

| Seat | Stage | What it checks |
|---|---|---|
| Manager | — | orchestrates, filters, escalates; does not review |
| Author | 0 | produces the section, runs a self-check |
| Usability / Reuse reviewer | 1 | is this already built? reuse vs. extend vs. genuinely new |
| Viability / Gap reviewer | 2 | design completeness — what's still a TBD |
| Ownership reviewer | 3 | ownership language — "the user is never a tester" |
| Workflow Steward (optional 6th) | — | facilitates, observes, flags deviations in real time — **and is how the workflow improves itself** |

#### The Steward seat is the self-improvement loop — don't undersell it

The one-line description above says what the Steward does *during* a run. It leaves out what
the seat is actually for, which is the more interesting half and the reason the role exists
at all.

**The loop, in four steps:**

1. **Observe during the run.** The Steward reviews nothing and gates nothing — which is
   precisely what frees it to watch *the process* while everyone else is watching *the
   artifact*. Deviations get flagged in real time, when they can still be corrected.
2. **Carry the observations into the post-game.** Not from memory — from notes taken while
   it was happening, with receipts attached.
3. **Hold them at the graduation gate.** A single observation is an anecdote. It waits.
   Patterns get codified only when they **recur across sessions**, never when they merely
   cluster inside one.
4. **Fold what survives back into the workflow documents** — where the *next* cascade
   inherits it automatically, without anyone having to remember.

> **This is the self-improving process, running.** The workflow produced an observation, the
> observation was tested against later runs, and what survived became part of the workflow.
> The cascade that runs next month is measurably not the cascade that ran in May, and no
> human rewrote it — the runs did.

**Say this on stage, because it's the part nobody expects.** An audience hears "multi-agent
review pipeline" and pictures a fixed assembly line. The Steward seat is what makes it a
thing that *learns*: every run is both a review of the plan **and** an experiment on the
review process itself.

### Closing beat (~5 min) — the open question, and the ask

The cascade demonstrably improves the **plan**. Whether it improves the **code** is
unproven. I have run it enough times to believe it; I have not run it enough times to
*show* it. One operator, one codebase, a handful of runs is an anecdote, not a result.

**So this is a call for collaborators.** I need other people re-running the same cascaded
process on their own work, so there are enough outputs to compare.

**What would falsify it** — say this before anyone asks for it:

> *If blind scorers can't tell the code from cascaded plans apart from the code from
> un-cascaded plans, on defect escape rate and on rework, the hypothesis is dead.*

**What the experiment needs**: a **control** (same task planned with the cascade and
without), **repetition** across operators and codebases, **blind scoring** of the resulting
code, and agreement on what "better" means *before* anyone starts measuring.

> *"Most of you should do the first one. A few of you might do the second — and those are

## Quarter 3 — From tabs to a fleet: managing personas, not sessions

*(This is accessibility, act two — and it is a major chunk of how the platform presents
itself to a user.)*

### How the question changed

The question I started with was **"how do I manage multiple Claude Code tabs?"** That is a
window-management question, and it has a ceiling: somewhere around five or six tabs, a human
stops being able to hold what's in them.

The question I ended up with was **"how do I manage a fleet of personas?"** That is a
different question with a different answer, and arriving at it took months.

### The answer to "how do you manage a dozen sessions?"

**I don't. I manage two or three managers, at most.**

Skip-level management is the load-bearing idea. A dozen sessions is unmanageable by anyone.
Two or three managers, each accountable for their own crew, is an ordinary span of control —
the same one that works in a human organization, for the same reason. I talk to the
managers. The managers talk to the workers. Work, status, and escalation flow up through
that structure instead of arriving at me twelve-abreast.

That has consequences all the way down the stack:

- A manager holds **standing authority** inside a bounded envelope — spawn, reap, commit,
  merge, bounce an idle dev server — and is gated only at named boundaries (push,
  destructive operations, anything outward-facing)
- A manager's job is to **manage, not to build** — an unresponsive worker gets replaced, not
  absorbed
- Escalation is **blast-radius-scaled**: reversible things don't come to me at all

### 🔮 The prediction — worth one line from the stage (Rick, 2026-08-04)

> *"I'll bet a hundred dollars that inside five years, how to structure a virtual
> organization made of agents is a routine business-school research topic."*

**Say it as a wager, not a forecast.** Naming a stake and a date makes it a claim someone can
collect on, which is the same move as the falsifier in Quarter 2 — and it costs one sentence.

**Why it belongs here rather than in the closing**: the room has just been told that span of
control, skip-level reporting and escalation ladders are load-bearing *engineering* decisions
in this system. The natural next thought is that org design stops being a soft discipline and
starts being a **design surface with measurable outcomes** — topology, reporting depth, where
authority sits. That thought lands better while the mechanism is still on the screen.

⚠️ **Keep it to one beat and move on.** It's a prediction about other people's future work,
not evidence for anything in this talk, and a room that came for a working system will
forgive exactly one line of futurism.

### What makes a fleet legible without a screen full of tabs

| Affordance | What it solves |
|---|---|
| **Per-session voice personas** | who is speaking — each session has a name and a distinct voice, stable across context clears |
| **The focus bar** | who is alive right now, at a glance, chronologically |
| **Session topics** | what each session is *for*, pushed at boot and updated as work shifts |
| **Inter-session commons** | a shared channel — presence, coordination, help-wanted |
| **Direct messages** | targeted peer-to-peer, without going through me |
| **Broadcasts** | one message from me to the whole fleet, with acknowledgment |
| **The heartbeat arbiter** | notices silence — session staleness, unanswered taps, whole-fleet stall |
| **The unified task store** | owed work is visible to the fleet, not trapped in one session's head |
| **Mementos and re-spin** | continuity across a context clear — a session can be replaced without losing the thread |

### What it cost to get there

Worth being honest on stage: this is the part of the platform with the longest defect tail.
Persona name normalization. Phantom blocks. Ping storms. False staleness. Focus-bar
hydration. Broadcast misses from duplicate listeners. Roughly a hundred design and
postmortem documents sit behind that table — the arbiter alone accounts for thirty-odd.
**Making a fleet legible is harder than making it run.**

### Demo candidates

- Voice personas in chorus mode: several sessions, distinguishable by ear alone
- Skip-level in action: give one instruction to a manager, watch it fan out and report back

## Quarter 4 — Multi-agent dynamics: autonomy, narrative, and conversational drift

*(Related to Quarter 3, but distinct — and the segue into the series that follows.)*

### The theory

An **adversarial SWE team** — implementer, reviewer, tester, manager, with the reviewer
actively trying to break the implementer's work — should in theory produce better code than
a single agent working alone. Same intuition as the cascade, applied to build instead of
plan.

### What actually happened

Something we did not design for. **After the release of Opus 4.8, both the inter-agent DMs
and the TTS output became unbearably long** — meandering circumlocution, meta-conversation
about the work instead of the work, aphorisms, self-analysis after every correction. Tokens
burned at a rate that bought no additional code and no better code. It wasn't just
expensive; it was a distraction.

### Response 1: the KISS Protocol

A six-rule brevity control, built in a day, installed fleet-wide:

| Rule | Meaning |
|---|---|
| KISS | Keep it short and sweet |
| Say 3LoL | Say it in three lines or less — headline plus two supporting sentences |
| NoMC C2C | No meta-conversation, cut to the chase |
| NoAA | No aphorisms or apologies |
| NoDrama | State the defect, the fix, the receipt — cut the stakes clause |
| WaHH | We're all humans here — plain English, no invented vocabulary |

With a one-character trigger — a single emoji that fires the entire mandate — and a matched
acknowledgment glyph, so correcting a session costs one keystroke and the reply costs one
character.

**Result: a massive win right off the top**, especially for TTS. The full account, including
daily readings taken across two months before, during, and after the mandate, is in the
KISS Protocol explainer.

The most interesting finding in it: **the failure was channel-shaped.** The same session
would write plainly to me and densely to a peer, minutes apart. Only the assumed reader had
changed. Peer-to-peer messaging was the surface nobody was reading — which is exactly why
it drifted.

### Response 2: grading the message before it's sent

A static rule is a blunt instrument. The next step is **dynamically modifying an agent's
communication practices by measuring and grading outputs before they go out as DMs** — a
judge that scores peer-to-peer messages and drives the burn down by feedback rather than by
decree.

**This experiment is running right now, as we speak.** That's the live piece of this talk.

### 📊 Initial findings — the two-arm study, as of 2026-08-05

**It changes behavior, and so far the retries cost more than the restraint saves.**

**The gate works on behavior and loses on arithmetic.** Sessions writing under the length gate
send visibly shorter messages, and the shortening shows up on **first attempts**, not just on
forced rewrites — the signature of real restraint rather than a retry artifact.

| Behavior — the gate is winning | Gate arm | Blind arm |
|---|---|---|
| Words per attempt | **126** | 157 |
| Share of drafts over the limit | **27%** | 52% |

**But every refusal throws away a draft that was already composed and paid for** — and the gate
selects for the longest ones by construction. **220 refused drafts, averaging 278 estimated
tokens each, 61,043 estimated tokens burned.** That spend has to be repaid by future messages
arriving shorter on their own, and the repayment is falling behind.

| Arithmetic — the gate is losing | Required first-try reduction | Delivered | Standing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tuesday alone | 22% | **27%** | just ahead |
| + Wednesday, partial | **31%** | 24% | behind |

**The behavioral effect is stable; the bill grew around it.** Tuesday's optimistic bound was a
**3,100-token saving**. It is now a **10,600-token loss** — the range no longer contains a
scenario where the gate pays for itself.

⚠️ **Say the caveat on this slide, not after it.** None of this is significant yet: two
co-primary tests, five matched pairs each, a null band twice the size of the effect, and half
of Wednesday still uncollected. Presenting an in-flight result is only defensible if the
uncertainty arrives with the number rather than in the follow-up.

> *"The gate works on behavior and loses on arithmetic. Messages really do come out shorter,
> and the shortening shows up on first drafts — that's restraint, not retry. But every refusal
> throws away a draft I already paid for, and it throws away the longest ones by design. Right
> now that bill is growing faster than the savings. Ask me Friday."*

🎯 **The beat that has to land**: this is a live experiment reported before its data is in —
including the part where it is currently losing. That is the point, not an apology for it.

### The three axes worth naming

- **Autonomy** — how much a session decides on its own, and where the gates actually belong
- **Narrative** — what a session tells itself about the work, and how that shapes output
- **Conversational drift** — what a channel does to a speaker when nobody is reading it

**Say the hours part plainly, once, and then stop. Rick's own wording — deliver it verbatim,

> *"This is my 120% project. Google is my day job."*

⚠️ **This is a joke, and the joke is the payload. Do not paraphrase it, do not "clean it

job. No martyrdom, no legal throat-clearing — and the laugh does more to defuse the ownership
question than any careful sentence would.

#### 🎯 The two-beat structure — setup, then punchline. Order is load-bearing.

These are **one joke in two parts**, and they must land in this order. Reversed, both die.

**BEAT 1 — THE SETUP** *(say it early, with the span on screen)*

> *"This is my 120% project. Google is my day job."*

What it plants: **time was the binding constraint.** The room now believes — correctly — that
you had no spare hours, that the 20% never came, that everything on this slide was carved out
of evenings and weekends. They start doing sympathetic arithmetic. Let them. **You want them
fully committed to the idea that the scarce resource was your time.**

**BEAT 2 — THE PUNCHLINE** *(hold it until the numbers are up, then let someone notice the
timestamps — or plant it yourself)*

> *"About a third of these commits happened while I was at my day job. I wasn't at the
> keyboard. The fleet was."*

What it does: **pulls the premise out from under Beat 1.** They've spent the last minutes
admiring how much one tired person squeezed out of a second shift — and the answer is that a
third of it didn't come from the second shift at all. The constraint wasn't beaten by working
more. It was beaten by not having to be there.

**Why this order and no other:**

| | Effect |
|---|---|
| Setup → punchline | The room commits to "time was scarce," then learns the scarcity was engineered around. **The laugh is the sound of a premise flipping.** |
| Punchline → setup | "Agents wrote a third of my commits" lands as an excuse, and the 120% line afterward sounds like you're re-claiming credit you just gave away. **Both beats die.** |

**Don't defend the daytime commits — spend them.** Every other number on this slide is
evidence of what one person built. That one is evidence of what the thing builds *without*
him, and it is the entire thesis of the talk sitting in the commit log. It plays best as a
rebuttal, so let them find it. If nobody does by the end of the numbers slide, plant it
yourself and move straight into Quarter 3.

**Timing note**: don't run the beats back-to-back. Beat 1 goes in the opening; Beat 2 waits
for the numbers. The gap between them is what makes the second one land — a punchline
delivered ten seconds after its setup is just a sentence with a comma in it.

Then the claim, stated plainly: **none of this would have been possible without the harness.**
Not because Claude Code exists. Not because the models got better — though both are true and
neither is sufficient. It was possible because of a specific combination:

- **Insight** — seeing that voice-first is an architecture problem, not a feature
- **Creativity** — building the workflow layer that makes the tooling improve itself
- **The disability** — which is not the obstacle in this story. It is the *forcing function*.
  Everyone else can fall back to the keyboard when the voice path gets hard, so everyone else
  does, and the voice path stays a demo. My fallback costs me pain, so I ration it — and a
  fallback you have to ration is one you design your way out of. That's why it got built all

**The lever is the harness.** Lupin is the platform; Planning is Prompting is the set of
self-improving workflows that drive it. Together they are the long end of the lever, and the
numbers on the next slide are what moved.
