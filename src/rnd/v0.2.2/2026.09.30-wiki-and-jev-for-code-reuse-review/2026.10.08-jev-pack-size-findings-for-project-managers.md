# Jev pack size: what we measured and what it means

**For:** project managers. **Reading time:** five minutes.
**Source:** the live stage one run of 2026-10-08. The final report is
`io/tmp/2026.10.08-maria-stage1-report-after-q1-rerun.txt`.

## The short version

- Sending Jev many code entries in one request ("packing") costs **less than half the tokens** of
  sending them one at a time, and finishes in **seconds instead of minutes**.
- Packs of **10** and packs of **50** gave answers as steady as asking the same question twice.
- Packs of **200** did not. They changed more borderline answers than a repeat run does, and an
  entry's answer depended on where in the pack it sat.
- The run's formal verdict is **"inconclusive"**, for one reason only: it found 69 borderline
  entries to judge by, and the plan asked for at least 90.
- **A decision is open:** which pack size the reuse sweep should use. It is Rick's to make.

## What we were testing

The code-reuse sweep asks Jev the same kind of question about every function in the codebase,
about 7,900 of them. There are two ways to ask:

| way | what it means |
|---|---|
| **Single** | One request per function. 7,943 requests. |
| **Packed** | Several functions in one request. A pack of 50 needs 159 requests. |

Packing is cheaper because the instructions are sent once per request, not once per function.
The risk is that Jev answers differently when a function shares a request with others. Stage one
measured both sides: the saving, and the risk.

We asked three different questions. For each, we ran the single way twice and the packed way at
three sizes: 10, 50 and 200.

## What packing saves

The cost was the same for all three questions, almost to the token.

| request shape | requests | tokens | share of single | time for one pass |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single | 7,943 | 4.04 million | 100% | 3.5 to 8 minutes |
| Packs of 10 | 794 | 1.97 million | 49% | about 1 minute |
| Packs of 50 | 159 | 1.79 million | 44% | about 13 seconds |
| Packs of 200 | 40 | 1.76 million | 43.5% | about 5 seconds |

Two things to notice:

1. **Most of the saving arrives at 10.** Going from single to packs of 10 halves the cost. Going
   from 10 to 200 saves only another 5 points.
2. **Bigger packs buy speed, not much money.** The token line is nearly flat from 50 upward.

Times are from question 1. A slowdown on our own side inflated later timings until it was fixed
during the run; it did not affect cost or answers.

## What packing risks

We cannot tell whether packing changed an answer just by comparing a packed run with a single run,
because **Jev does not give exactly the same answer twice even when nothing changes**. So every
comparison is made against that background wobble, which we call the noise floor. We measured it
by running the single shape twice.

The entries that matter are the borderline ones: functions whose score sits close to a cut-off, so
a small shift moves them from "yes" to "no". We counted how many of those flipped.

| pack size | score drift | borderline entries flipped | flipped by a plain repeat run | position in the pack matters? | result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | within noise | 9 | 9 | not tested | passed both tests |
| 50 | within noise | 8 | 9 | not tested | passed both tests |
| 200 | within noise | **12** | 9 | **yes** | **failed two tests** |

In plain terms:

- **Packs of 10 and 50 are as steady as asking twice.** They flipped no more borderline answers
  than a repeat run did.
- **Packs of 200 are less steady than asking twice.** They flipped more borderline answers, and
  moving the same function to a different place in the pack changed its score by up to 0.13 on a
  scale of 0 to 1. Thirteen points is enough to move a borderline function across a cut-off.
- **On the ordinary, non-borderline entries, every size agreed with single.** For all three sizes,
  99 entries in every 100 moved by 0.02 or less, which is the same as the noise floor. The trouble
  with 200 shows up only near the cut-offs, which is exactly where the sweep makes its decisions.

## How size changes the outcome, in one picture

| as the pack grows from 10 to 200 | what happens |
|---|---|
| cost | falls a little: 49% to 43.5% of single |
| speed | rises a lot: a minute down to seconds |
| steadiness on clear-cut entries | no change |
| steadiness on borderline entries | holds at 10 and 50, breaks at 200 |
| dependence on position in the pack | appears at 200; not tested at 10 or 50 |

The pattern is a familiar one: the last few percent of saving cost a disproportionate amount of
reliability. Somewhere between 50 and 200 the answers start to depend on the company an entry
keeps. We did not test sizes between 50 and 200, so we do not know where that point is.

## Why the report says "inconclusive"

The plan set a rule before the run: a pack size is only declared safe if the three questions
together supply at least **90** borderline entries to judge by. The run found **69** (38, 11 and
20 from the three questions).

So 10 and 50 passed every test that ran, on a sample about a quarter smaller than the plan wanted.
The report labels that honestly as inconclusive instead of rounding it up to a pass. Packs of 200
failed outright, and a larger sample would not rescue a failure.

## The decision that is open

| option | what you get | what you give up |
|---|---|---|
| **Packs of 50** | The cheapest size that passed. 44% of the tokens, 159 requests. | Accepting a sample of 69 where the plan asked for 90. Its worst single score shift (0.23) was larger than for packs of 10 (0.16). |
| **Packs of 10** | The same pass results with a smaller worst-case shift. | About 4.5 points more tokens and five times as many requests as 50, for no measured gain in steadiness. |
| **One more question first** | Enough borderline entries to make the verdict formal. | About 12 million more tokens and a short delay; a fourth question has to exist or be written. |
| **Stay with single** | No packing risk at all. | More than twice the tokens, against two sizes that passed. |

The manager seat's recommendation is packs of 50. That is a judgement on the evidence above, not a
formal pass, and it is recorded as such.

## What this does not tell us

- **Three questions, one codebase, one evening.** The saving was identical across the three
  questions; the steadiness was not. Question 3 showed packing flipping somewhat more borderline
  entries than a repeat run at every size, where question 2 showed none.
- **One model version.** Every request was answered by `jev-1.13.0`. A new version could behave
  differently, and the run stops itself if the version changes underneath it.
- **Position was only tested at 200.** We do not know whether 50 is position-sensitive; it passed
  the tests it was given.
- **Question 3 ran on 7,952 entries**, nine more than the other two, because the code changed
  mid-run. Any figure pooled across all three mixes two slightly different sets.
- **The whole stage cost 45.9 million tokens** of a 71 million allowance: 46 runs, about $1.93 at
  the account's rate (ledger read 2026-10-08 19:55 EDT).

## Words used here

| word | meaning |
|---|---|
| entry | one function or method from the codebase, described for Jev |
| pack | several entries sent in one request |
| noise floor | how much Jev's answer moves when the identical question is asked again |
| borderline (boundary) entry | an entry whose score is close enough to a cut-off that a small shift changes the decision |
| flip | an entry that lands on the other side of a cut-off |
