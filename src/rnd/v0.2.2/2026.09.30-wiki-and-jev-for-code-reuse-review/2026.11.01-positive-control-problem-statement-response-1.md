# Failed Positive Control in the Code-Reuse Checker: Bias Diagnosis, Fix Selection, and Pass Criteria

The control as run is not a fair test of the checker. The planted rewrites never went through the production indexing pipeline, so they were indexed with less text than any real entry gets. A control that skips steps real items go through measures how the control was built, not how the system behaves. The right fix is a new option E: run the exact production indexer, including purpose-statement generation, on the plants. Option A (hand-written docstrings) is second best, B is a diagnostic only, C is not recommended, and D should be rejected. The all-or-nothing pass mark should be replaced with tiered, pre-registered thresholds based on binomial error rates.

## TL;DR

- **Not fair as it stands.** The plants are missing the purpose statement that about 99% of real entries carry (12,615 of 12,713 are in the prose list). They are also missing the text the vector list embeds. The control therefore measures the plants' "un-indexedness" as much as the checker's recall. Analytical chemistry defines spike recovery as recovery after the spike is "subjected to the entire analytical procedure"; this control fails that definition.\[1\]
- **Fix: Option E, not A.** Re-index the plants with the production indexer and its purpose-statement generator (about 20–40 calls). Log the generated text and audit it for leakage against the needs. Add H, a zero-cost ablation of vector rank with and without the purpose line. Also check whether each pair's original is found for its need, which isolates need quality from plant indexing. B is a useful ablation but tests a different system. C introduces selection bias. D reports a number with the wrong meaning.
- **A 100% gate on 17–20 hard pairs is miscalibrated.** A checker with a true 95% recall passes 20/20 only 36% of the time (0.95^20). Over two passes (about 36 trials) that falls to 16%. Use tiers instead: an easy sanity tier that halts the trial on 2 or more misses out of 20, and a work-alike tier reported as recall@128 with a Wilson interval, gated on a pre-registered floor. Count the pairs that failed setup in an ITT-style denominator and report them separately.

So something real is depressing search-stage recall. The open question is whether it is the checker or the control.
- All 12 misses happened at search, and the judge never rejected a plant (9/9). The failure therefore belongs to stage 1, and the judge's performance on plants cannot yet be assessed: 9 trials only bound it at ≥0.70.

### 2. Why the plant is structurally disadvantaged in fusion

**Missing from a list means zero contribution.** In reciprocal rank fusion (Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher, SIGIR 2009) the score is the sum of 1/(k+rank) over lists, with k=60, the value the original work found gave the best average result across its benchmarks. A document absent from a list gets nothing from it. Redis's hybrid-search guide works through the arithmetic: a document ranked first by keyword search but missing from the vector results scores about 0.016, and loses to documents that both retrievers merely agree on. Score-fusion variants have the same problem in a different form. Bruch, Gai & Ingber (ACM TOIS 2023) note that a missing score must be imputed before fusion.\[2\] They also find RRF sensitive to its parameters and a tuned convex combination better.\[3\] CombMNZ, which multiplies by the number of lists that returned the document, would penalize a 2-list item more sharply still.

**A worked example (RRF, k=60).** Take a missed plant at vector rank 65 and code rank 100.
- Its score is 1/125 + 1/160 = 0.0143.
- An entry that ranks about 150th in all three lists scores the same.
- The best any 2-list item can score is 2/61 = 0.033. A 3-list item reaches that at a uniform rank of about 31.
- Among about 12,700 entries, many will be middling in all three lists, so a plant that is only moderately good in two lists falls outside the top 128.
- The observed found ranks (vector 6–210) and missed ranks (vector 65–8,800) fit this picture.
- This is an illustration only: the real fusion method is not stated and has to be checked in the code.

**The prose-list gap is the smaller half of the bias. The weakened vector text is probably the larger half.**
- The vector list embeds name + signature + docstring + purpose line. For a plant, that is reduced to name + signature.
- The code-search literature shows NL→code retrievers lean heavily on exactly the text the plants lack. SACL (Gupta et al., Findings of EMNLP 2025) finds retrievers "heavily rely on surface-level textual features" and are biased toward well-documented code.\[4\]\[5\]
- CodeXGLUE's AdvTest shows the effect of identifiers. Replacing function and variable names with placeholders cut Python code-search MRR from 0.809 to 0.419 (RoBERTa) and from 0.869 to 0.507 (CodeBERT).\[6\]\[7\]
- The dry run fits this. When the needs contained the original's name, 17/20 were found.

**Retrievability framing.** Azzopardi & Vinay (CIKM 2008) define retrievability as how easily a document can be retrieved under a given system. Retrieval bias is the systematic inequality of that quantity across documents.\[8\]\[9\] "Undocumented and un-summarized code is less retrievable" is a known property of this kind. It is a real property of the checker, but only for entries that are actually indexed that way in production.

### 3. The key question: is the purpose statement generated by the indexer?

The evidence says almost certainly yes, and that changes the decision.

- **Coverage is too high for hand-written text.** The prose list covers 12,615 of 12,713 entries (99.2%). Hand-written documentation is nowhere near that dense in real code. In the CodeSearchNet paper (Husain et al., arXiv 1909.09436), only 2,326,976 of 6,452,446 collected functions (about 36%) had documentation. A 99% rate for "docstring or purpose line" is very likely produced by a generator.
- **Industry practice is to generate this text at index time.**
  - Qodo's "RAG for a Codebase with 10k Repos" pipeline uses LLMs to write a natural-language description of every code chunk, then embeds those descriptions alongside the code.
  - Greptile argues for translating code to English before embedding. In its blog post "Codebases are uniquely hard to search semantically", one example query scored 0.8152 cosine similarity against an English description versus 0.7280 against the code itself, about 12% higher (one anecdote, not a benchmark).
- **Academic results show the effect is large.**
  - Code-Craft/HCGS (Cisco, arXiv 2504.08975) writes LLM summaries for every function over 7,531 functions in 5 codebases. On one codebase it reports up to 82% relative improvement in top-1 precision over code-only retrieval.\[10\]
  - SACL's LLM-generated descriptions raised Recall@1 on HumanEval from 54.3 to 69.5 when function names were hidden, and from 18.9 to 31.7 when all text features were hidden. With documentation intact there was no gain (98.8 → 98.8).\[5\]
  - "Retrieval-Oriented Code Representations" (arXiv 2607.11046) reports role-aware LLM file summaries beating file paths by up to 40% Hit@5. Fusing representations adds up to 31.9%.\[11\]
  - ReCo (Li et al., ACL 2024) summarizes and regenerates corpus code offline to close the style gap.\[12\]

**What follows.** If the purpose line is pipeline-generated:
- Real undocumented code does get a purpose line and does appear in the prose list. Only about 98 entries (0.8%) do not.
- The problem statement's caveat that real undocumented code is also absent from the prose list, so 5/17 may be fair, is then largely false.
- Option D's reading ("recall on undocumented work-alikes") is then wrong. 5/17 is recall on entries that skipped indexing, a population that does not exist in production.

This is the matrix-matching principle. FDA's chemical method validation guidance defines spike recovery after the spike is "subjected to the entire analytical procedure." It also says recovery should be computed "for the method as written."\[1\] ERCC RNA spike-ins give a cautionary example from biology: control transcripts showed control-specific deviations under poly-A selection that tracked transcript length.\[13\] When a spike-in differs from native material at a processing step, recovery measures that difference.

### 4. Work-alikes are a legitimate hardest tier, but they should not be the only tier

The clone-detection literature places semantic (Type-4) clones at the hard end of the scale.
- Zhang & Saber (University of Galway, ICCQ 2024; arXiv 2407.02402) tested 300 samples per clone type and defined Type-4 as pairs with under 50% similarity. On BigCloneBench, GPT-4 reached a Type-4 recall of only 0.23, against 0.94 for existing clone-detection techniques. Both GPT models did better on LLM-generated clones than on human-written ones.
- GPTCloneBench reports that conventional clone-detection tools' recall falls as Type-4 content rises.\[14\]

The checker's task is harder still. It does not compare code to code. It goes code → LLM-written need → retrieval → rewritten code, and every hop loses information. A recall well below 1.0 on this tier is expected even for a good system. That is why a 100% gate on this tier alone guarantees false alarms, and why a positive control also needs an easy tier that a working system essentially never misses.

### 5. Synthetic needs and the leak check

- Benchmark construction has dealt with identifier leakage before. CodeSearchNet removes the docstring from the target code. AdvTest normalizes identifiers to keep the task from being solved lexically.\[15\]\[16\] So the leak check is good practice.
- The synonym substitution ("slider" → "range-picker") has a cost. It removes exactly the lexical overlap that BM25 and identifier-heavy embedders rely on (see the AdvTest drop above). The resulting needs may be harder than real developer queries, which often reuse domain vocabulary.
- Synthetic-query work uses round-trip filtering as standard practice. Promptagator keeps a generated query only if its source passage comes back in the top K. Doc2Query-- shows that removing hallucinated expansions improved effectiveness by up to 16%.\[17\]\[18\]
- Recommended adaptation:
  - Check answerability against the original using a reference retriever that is not the system under test, such as a different embedder.
  - Never filter on the plant, which would be circular.
- The 3/20 and 2/20 draft failures are an artefact of over-strict leak rules. Report them; do not silently drop them.

### 6. Score the two stages separately

Fusion only reorders the union of candidates. It cannot surface a document that no retriever found (Qdrant hybrid-search documentation).\[19\] The standard breakdown has three parts:
- stage-1 recall@128, the ceiling;
- judge recall given the target is in the shortlist;
- end-to-end recall, which equals the product of the two.

Report all three. In this run they would be about 0.26 (9/35), 1.0 (9/9), and 0.26. The headline failure is therefore entirely a search-stage, and probably indexing-stage, problem.

## Evaluation of Options

| Option | Verdict | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **A** Hand-write docstring + purpose line (~160 calls) | Second best; only if E is impossible | Fixes the coverage gap. But a docstring is text production would *not* have for undocumented code, so it over-corrects, and authored text can leak the need's vocabulary. Not matrix-matched. |
| **B** Drop the prose list for all entries | Diagnostic only | Tests a different checker. Useful as an ablation to bound how much the prose list matters, but it cannot certify the production system. It also leaves the weakened vector text unfixed. |
| **C** Plant documented code / new pairs | Not recommended | Selection bias toward easy, documented items. Breaks the existing labels. Costs more calls. Does not test the indexing path. |
| **D** Accept 5/17 and finish | Reject | If the purpose line is generated, 5/17 is recall on a population that does not exist in production. The CI (0.13–0.53) is also too wide to be useful. |
| **E (new)** Run the exact production indexer on the plants (purpose line generated, docstring left empty), log the text | **Recommended** | Matrix-matched: the plant goes through "the entire analytical procedure." Cheap (about 1–2 calls per plant). Leakage becomes auditable instead of authored. |
| **F (new)** Tiered control | Recommended | Separates "system broken" from "hard cases are hard." |
| **G (new)** Stage-separated scoring | Recommended | Required for attributing misses; nearly free. |
| **H (new)** Vector-rank ablation | Recommended | Measures what the problem statement says was not measured: re-embed each plant with and without the generated purpose line and compare ranks. Zero LLM calls apart from E's. |
| **I (new)** Original-as-known-item check | Recommended, zero calls if logs exist | The original is in the index with full text. If originals reach the shortlist for their own needs while plants do not, the needs are fine and indexing is to blame. If originals also miss, the needs or the leak check are to blame. |

**Leakage audit for E.**
- For each plant, compute token and subtoken overlap (and embedding similarity) between the need and the plant's generated purpose line.
- Compare that with the same overlap between the need and the original's production purpose line.
- The plant's line should not be systematically closer to the need than the original's own line.
- Some vocabulary convergence is legitimate. Two LLMs describing the same behaviour will share words, and production has the same property.

## Recommendations

**Rerun plan and budget.** This assumes one judge call per need per run; replays reuse frozen drafts.

| Step | Model calls (est.) |
|---|---|
| I: original known-item check from existing logs | 0 |
| E: production purpose-line generation for 20 plants (allowing retries) | 20–40 |
| H: embed plant ± purpose line, rank comparison | 0 LLM (embedding only) |
| Tier 0/1 controls (see below), 20 pairs each, judge calls | ~40 |
| Tier 2 rerun on frozen drafts, 2 passes | ~40 |
| Fix the 3–5 failed drafts with a relaxed leak rule (≤4 tries) | ≤20 |
| B as an ablation, 1 pass, replayed | ~20 |
| Expand Tier 2 by about 20 new pairs (rewrite + need + run, ~6 calls each) | ~120 |
| **Total** | **~260–300 of 669**, leaving a reserve of over 350 |

**Tiers and pass marks (pre-register before running).**
- **Tier 0, known item.** The original itself, with its production index entry, against its need. This catches broken needs or a broken index. Halt the trial if 2 or more of 20 are missed.
  - If true recall is 0.98, a false halt happens about 6% of the time.
  - If true recall is 0.85, the halt fires about 82% of the time.
- **Tier 1, alpha-renamed copy.** The original with identifiers renamed, docstring stripped, and indexed by the production indexer. Same rule: halt on 2 or more misses out of 20. This tier tests the indexing path for undocumented code without semantic distance.
- **Tier 2, work-alike rewrite indexed via E.** Do not halt on this tier. Report recall@128 and end-to-end recall with Wilson CIs, and gate on a pre-registered floor.
  - With 20 evaluable pairs and a cut of 16 or more found, true recall 0.9 passes about 96% of the time and true recall 0.6 passes only about 5%.
  - Telling 0.9 from 0.75 needs about 40 or more pairs. That is the case for the expansion.
- **Unevaluable pairs.** Use ITT-style reporting.
  - The primary analysis uses evaluable pairs.
  - Also report the all-pairs figure with draft failures counted as misses.
  - List every exclusion with its reason, as CONSORT asks for post-randomisation exclusions.\[20\] Montedori et al. (Trials 2011) found that trials labelled "modified ITT" were more likely to exclude participants after randomisation (adjusted OR 3.43), which is the reason for explicit exclusion accounting.\[21\]
  - Never replace a failed pair after seeing results.

**Decision rule after the rerun.**
- If Tier 2 recall under E is about 0.6 or higher and H shows large rank gains from the purpose line, the original failure was mostly a control artefact. Proceed.
- If Tier 2 stays at about 0.3 or below under E while Tiers 0 and 1 pass, the checker genuinely struggles with work-alikes. That is a real finding about the product, not a control failure. Consider fixes such as a larger shortlist, convex-combination fusion, or summary-weighted vectors.

## Claims in the Problem Statement to Re-measure or Correct

1. **"Real undocumented code is also absent from the prose list."** Probably false. 99.2% coverage implies generated purpose lines. Check the indexer code and count entries whose docstring is empty but which have a purpose line.
2. **"Fusion is RRF-like."** Must be confirmed. CombMNZ or min-max CombSUM would change the size of the penalty, and Bruch et al. show fusion behaviour depends on these choices.\[3\]
3. **"Vague needs ruled out."** Word counts do not establish answerability. Use check I (whether the original is retrieved) as the actual test.
4. **"Word overlap with rewrite name/signature doesn't separate found vs missed."** That fits the hypothesis that the missing text, not the identifiers, drove the misses. The relevant overlap is between the need and the original's purpose line.
5. **The implied 100% pass mark.** It is statistically incoherent for n≈20 hard items (see the TL;DR).
6. **The dry run's 17/20.** It is not a valid baseline because the needs contained the name. It is a useful upper bound for lexical leakage.

## What the Outside Experts Will Likely Ask

- Show the indexer: is the purpose line generated, by which model and prompt, and was the need-drafter the same model? Same-model convergence inflates recall.
- What is the fusion function and what is k? Are lists truncated before fusion?
- Where did the originals rank for their own needs?
- Was the index really identical across passes? Was the embedding model deterministic?
- Why are there 8 Python and 12 TS pairs, given the different Python (1/6) and TS (3–4/11–12) results? Is the split large enough to say anything per language? (No: Python 1/6 has a Wilson CI of about 0.03–0.56.)
- Would a larger shortlist (256 or 512) recover the misses, and what would that cost the judge?
- How independent are the two passes, given that they share pairs?

## Caveats

- Several conclusions rest on an inference, that purpose lines are generated, which must be verified in code before committing to E.
- The fusion example assumes RRF with k=60.
- Literature effect sizes come from different tasks: file-level localization, HumanEval and MBPP snippets, Java and Python corpora. They show direction and rough magnitude, not a prediction for this index.
- Some sources are vendor blogs (Greptile, Qodo) with no benchmarks.
- The budget estimates assume one judge call per run. Rescale them if the checker makes multiple calls per need.

## Strongest applicable findings

### 1. Requiring a second equivalent implementation is probably the wrong prerequisite for mining ordinary reuse needs

A realistic need plus one independently verified existing implementation already supports a useful discovery test. Searching for additional valid implementations is a separate answer-set expansion problem.

Your earlier trial spent 673 comparisons obtaining three positive judgments. That measures yield from a particular candidate-selection-and-judging pipeline. It does **not** establish that useful reuse opportunities are rare, or even that duplicates are rare.

The earlier review’s proposed “BM25 misses” stratum is useful, but its interpretation needs correction: **lexical retrieval difficulty is not semantic implementation diversity**. Selecting all hard examples through BM25 failure would also handicap that baseline by construction. Keep this as a labeled challenge subset alongside an unselected sample.

### 2. Missing prose is plausible, but the representation problem may be more fundamental

As described, the dense representation contains names, signatures, docstrings, and purpose statements—**not the implementation body**. For an undocumented rewrite, your semantic route therefore receives little evidence of its behavior. This deserves a raw-code embedding baseline before concluding that documentation alone is the remedy.

Also, **99% prose-index coverage does not establish that 99% of competitors receive useful third-route votes for a particular query**. You need actual returned ranks, list truncation, treatment of zero BM25 scores, and fusion contributions. If zero-score entries receive ranks and votes, that is a separate implementation concern.

SACL makes the missing-documentation hypothesis credible, while also warning that documentation can attract retrieval without being correct. Generated purpose text should be treated as an index feature whose accuracy and benefit require measurement—not as evidence that the code satisfies the need. [SACL](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.1365.pdf)

### 3. The positive labels need reconciliation before the positive control can be trusted

The October 31 companion statement reports that **both human raters judged three of the twenty synthetic pairs not to perform the same job**. The November 1 statement calls all twenty work-alikes. These may have been repaired or re-adjudicated, but the supplied material does not establish that.

Replace the broad “same job” label with a contract: accepted inputs, required outputs, exceptions, state changes, side effects, and allowed adaptation. Distinguish:

- Directly reusable.
- Reusable with a small, specified adapter.
- Helpful example but not a substitute.
- Incompatible or uncertain.

Differential execution can disprove equivalence with a counterexample. Passing generated tests cannot prove equivalence across all relevant inputs. GPTCloneBench’s multi-stage validation is a better precedent than trusting a rewrite instruction alone. [GPTCloneBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13963)

### 4. Identifier removal is an adversarial condition, not a complete leakage policy

Real developers legitimately mention library names, APIs, domain terminology, and error strings. Replacing “slider” with “range-picker” may make a query less representative without making its semantics more trustworthy. Query length and behavior counts do not establish realism.

Separate three conditions: authentic caller language; a natural paraphrase without answer-specific names; and deliberate identifier suppression. Generate indexing summaries without access to evaluation queries. Split evaluation by task or function family so closely related originals, rewrites, and paraphrases cannot straddle tuning and test sets.

CodeSearchNet’s separate real-query challenge and documentation-derived corpus illustrate why query provenance matters. [CodeSearchNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09436)

### 5. An incomplete answer set is manageable; pretending it is complete is not

For your product, distinguish **finding any usable implementation** from **recovering every known equivalent**, and from **finding the particular planted rewrite**. The original function remains indexed: finding it may satisfy the developer while failing the planted-target control. Report both outcomes.

Pool candidates from different retrieval methods, adjudicate newly returned answers, and retain “unjudged” separately from “irrelevant.” Foundational research shows that incomplete judgments can distort metrics; later TREC experiments also show that sufficiently diverse, deep pools can preserve system rankings remarkably well. Neither result licenses assuming that a shallow pool is exhaustive. [Buckley & Voorhees, 2004](https://www.nist.gov/publications/retrieval-evaluation-incomplete-information), [Voorhees, Soboroff & Lin, 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11086)

Two models agreeing helps triage. It does not remove shared errors or the circularity of using the production judge to select its own evaluation positives.

### 6. Separate plumbing checks, retrieval capability, judge accuracy, and agent behavior

“All twelve misses occurred before judging” localizes those failures well. “The judge rejected none” only describes the five retrieved positives; it says little about harder positives or false acceptance of near-misses.

Use unequivocal fixtures for a strict plumbing gate. Treat undocumented semantic rewrites as a capability stress test with uncertainty, rather than demanding perfection twice. Even **20/20 independent successes** would only bound the failure rate below approximately **13.9% at one-sided 95% confidence**, assuming representative sampling and valid labels. Repeating overlapping pairs does not double the independent sample size.

Finally, your ultimate outcome remains fewer unnecessary implementations with correctness preserved. RepoExec and RepoReuse show why passing tests and retrieving code cannot substitute for measuring actual adoption. [RepoExec](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-naacl.82/), [RepoReuse](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.35357v1)

## Three prioritized experiments

These are proposed designs, not established findings.

### 1. Diagnose representation and fusion on frozen, verified controls

First reconcile the twenty labels and freeze the saved queries. Put every planted entry through the same ingestion path as production code. Compare four conditions: no generated prose; prose in dense retrieval only; prose in BM25 only; prose in both. Generate descriptions from the rewrite and permitted repository context, without seeing the need or original description.

Add a code-body embedding baseline. Replay dense-only, lexical-only, current fusion, and fusion without the prose route using cached results. Record per-route scores, ranks, fusion contributions, and target presence at 10, 32, and 128 candidates. Keep the shortlist size fixed for the primary comparison; measure larger lists separately.

**Decision:** distinguish missing behavioral representation, missing lexical evidence, and fusion demotion. Adopt option A only if ordinary ingestion produces useful, accurate descriptions and gains survive a separate real-code sample. Option B is valuable as an ablation, not automatically a production fix.

### 2. Build a small, realistic need set without duplicate mining as an admission rule

Start with roughly **60 needs**, explicitly a pilot rather than a precision target: half from pre-search agent/developer requests or historical issue/caller context; half derived from existing code for a controlled comparison. Historical answers must already exist at the chosen repository snapshot. Balance Python and TypeScript within both sources.

Accept one verified answer initially. Expand answer sets from the union of lexical, code-embedding, purpose-embedding, and structural/call-site retrieval. Preserve genuine unresolved or no-answer requests rather than generating code to force a match. Use blind review of behavioral contracts, plus executable checks where practical.

Measure usable items per reviewer-hour and model call, query realism, answer ambiguity, and **any-valid-answer Hit@128**. Report natural and generated queries separately.

**Decision:** determine whether the cheaper construction process yields credible evaluation items and whether synthetic queries materially overstate or understate discovery quality. Do not scale to 400 until that comparison is understood.

### 3. Calibrate a cheaper judge and connect discovery to actual reuse

Construct a blinded audit set containing verified positives, semantically close wrong answers, uncertain cases, and a probability sample of routine accepted **and rejected** candidates. Oversample disagreements for diagnosis, but preserve sampling probabilities so aggregate error estimates are not biased.

Compare cached single-model labels, two-model agreement, and selective human escalation. Report false acceptance and false rejection separately by language and documentation status. For an isolated judge test, explicitly supply verified candidates regardless of retrieval rank; then measure the complete pipeline on normal retrieval outputs.

On a small held-out task sample, compare agents with and without the checker. Track candidate retrieved, candidate read, valid implementation called, correctness, and newly introduced duplication. Treat this as a feasibility pilot; size a confirmatory study from the observed variation and a meaningful minimum benefit.

**Decision:** choose the least expensive labeling process whose audited errors are acceptable, and establish whether better discovery actually changes agent behavior. The supplied trial has no human labels; until an independent audit exists, model-only results should remain diagnostic rather than a trustworthy population recall claim.
